Structural controls

every objective breeds its parasite

A genetic algorithm does not share your goals; it shares your fitness function. Give it any objective with a weakness, and it will find the weakness — not sometimes, reliably. This section collects the full lineage of exploits the searches invented and the structural controls each one forced, because the trustworthiness of everything in this essay rests on them:

Boundary sniffing, live

The last parasite deserves its own exhibit, because it was the first to defeat cross-radius verification. During the ablation runs, one batch on edge-adjacency patches returned "gliders" with travel 48 and max population 5 — at generation 1, at every radius. The champion genome, in full: * | n₀ ≥ 3 → 0; * | otherwise → 1. It propagates nothing. On an empty board every interior tile has three or more dead neighbours, so it stays dead — but the corner tiles of an edge-adjacency patch have degree ≤ 2 and cannot, so they ignite. The rule does not travel to the boundary; it detects the boundary, and since every verification patch has one, the exploit scales perfectly. Here it is, exactly as evolved:

The fix is the causality filter: zero fitness for any run whose activity leaves the seed's light cone — travel bounded by seed extent plus generations × fan margin. In the panel above, the cone is drawn: the faintly lightened disc around the seed is the region physics can have reached, growing one ring per generation — and the corner cells ignite hopelessly outside it. The sniffer violates the filter 48-fold at generation 1. Structurally, the exploit cannot exist on vertex patches (minimum vertex degree exceeds the condition-threshold cap), and none of the committed glider rules boundary-sniff — they are vacuum-stable, verified by trajectory. The ablation

The hunt claimed two ingredients made the difference — neighbour-visible states with conjunctions, and vertex adjacency. A causal claim wants a 2×2 grid, so the two unexplored cells were searched at the full calibrated budget (256 × 800, two independent seeds × both families per cell, each cell driven by its native machinery):

edge adjacencyvertex adjacency
Generations (k = 4)mortal travellers, 43–58 rings, then deathmortal travellers, 45–58 rings, then death
table rulesmortal travellers and oscillators, 31–47 ringsgliders, routinely (4 of 6 runs)

Both axes are individually necessary at this budget; only the pair suffices. Generations rules fail on either neighbourhood — the neighbour-invisibility of their dying states limits them even with the wider vertex fan. Table rules fail on edge adjacency — neighbour-visible signalling is not enough without it. Two details give the grid its weight. The Generations-edge cell ran at 13× the historic search budget, so the mortal era's null was not underpowered after all. And all three null cells hit the same ceiling — thirty to sixty rings of mortal travel, then death or a trap — the exact wall that defined the era. Only the (vertex, table) cell crosses from mortal to immortal. Caveats as ever: four runs per cell at one budget is bounded evidence, not proof, and the drivers are asymmetric by design.

That is the case for trusting the empirical claims: not that the searches were honest, but that they were caught cheating in every way they tried, and the controls that caught them are structural. The final question is different in kind — what would it take to need no trust at all? A glider that provably flies forever. That is the last section but one.
