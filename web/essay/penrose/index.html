Validation on Penrose

the control experiment: reproduce the one native glider

One glider has been exhibited natively on an aperiodic tiling: Goucher, 2012, on the Penrose rhombs — hand-engineered in the Ready simulator community against Trevorrow's conjecture that none could exist, and never found by automated search. (Bailey and Lindsey took a different route, building automata on Penrose tilings isomorphic to Life itself — Life's glider imported wholesale, not an object of the board's own physics.) Goucher's glider lives outside everything the last three sections swept, on precisely the two axes this project fixed at the outset. Its neighbourhood is vertex adjacency — tiles are neighbours if they share so much as a corner; all our graphs were edge. And its rule is no Generations rule: four states — ground, head, tail, wing — governed by a priority-ordered table whose conditions read the tail and wing counts and demand conjunctions (a birth requires n₁ ≥ 1 and n₂ ≥ 1). In Generations, non-firing states are invisible to neighbours; here they are the signalling medium.

A negative result is worth only as much as the pipeline behind it. So the method rule points at us: before "no glider on the monotiles" can be trusted, the same pipeline must first detect the known glider on its home board, and then rediscover one blind. The machinery: Penrose P2 (kites and darts) and P3 (rhombs) as substrate families; vertex adjacency computed in tiling-core by bucketing the exact-arithmetic placement vertices, no epsilon anywhere — the engine still consumes a plain adjacency graph; and TableRule, priority rows over per-state neighbour counts, strictly containing the Generations family (pinned by an encoding-equivalence test). The patch machinery of The board — addresses, legal roots and all — worked on the new tilings unchanged.

The table, as printed, cannot glide

Encoding Goucher's Table 1 literally produces a rule in which no row ever outputs state 1. A head can move only by being reborn ahead of itself; with no row producing heads, the published seed dies at generation 4, in every orientation:

Row 2's next-state is a typo — the printed 3 must be a 1, by Goucher's own prose ("a new head is reborn ahead of the original one") and by the Ready reference implementation, whose kernel reads n₁>0 ∧ n₃≥2 → 1. As printed, nothing moves; with the 1 restored, everything in the rest of this section runs.

Here is the corrected table in full — the priority-table shape of The game, over four states (0 ground, 1 head, 2 tail, 3 wing; n₁ = head neighbours, and so on). First matching row applies; no match means ground. The repaired digit is bold:

Read it as a relay. A head beside wings becomes a tail; a tail always becomes a wing; wings and everything else fade to ground — and the two birth rows are where the geometry enters: ground touching head and tail grows wing, ground touching head and two wings grows the new head, one step further along the ribbon. Every condition is a conjunction of two different state-counts; nothing in the Generations family can say any row of this.

Same rule, different tiling: the loopers

The glider's immortality is geometric: it rides a de Bruijn ribbon — a chain of rhombi crossed edge-to-parallel-edge — and because rhombi are parallelograms, progress along a ribbon is monotone. No loops, ever. Kites and darts are not parallelograms, so on P2 the identical rule turns every trajectory into a closed orbit — and Goucher's paper publishes the exact periods, which makes P2 the perfect kill-condition for our classifier:

Twenty seed orientations at five anchors reproduce Periodic{20}, Periodic{40} — "the most common eventuality", as Goucher puts it — and, at radius 64, Periodic{200}: 40 and 200 open the published decagonal sequence 40, 200, 1240, …, whose successive periods obey Goucher's recurrence Pn+2 = 5Pn+1 + 6Pn (5·200 + 6·40 = 1240). Exact recurrences (Brent's cycle detection on full states), not estimates. The level-1 detection control passes end to end.

The blind rediscovery

Level 2 hands the unmodified search strategy the right Penrose substrate and rule family and asks it to find a glider from nothing: a GA over table-rule genomes (up to 8 priority rows, two conditions each, k = 4), random initialisation, a seed bank that presupposes nothing about which state is head or tail, and the standard travel × flatness objective. At the scale of the monotile searches it fails — champions plateau at fitness 26–29 against an available 45 (a true glider's score on this patch). Seeding one run with Goucher's rule (deliberately not blind) scores 45 immediately: the fitness landscape has the glider at its summit, so what is being tested is purely whether the optimizer can climb to it. Scaled to a GA population of 256 genomes for 800 GA generations, it can: fitness sat on a plateau near 15 for four hundred GA generations, then jumped to 45 — ≈115,000 genome evaluations, ≈1.5 million classified runs, into the search. And the needle it found was not the needle we knew about: the discovered rule is a new glider, not Goucher's.

A menagerie, not a miracle

Four of six independent scaled runs produced a cross-radius-verified glider, and they are mechanically diverse — P3 hosts at least three distinct classes: Goucher's rail-rider above, and two the search produced:

The transport statistics separate the classes cleanly. The phoenix is exactly ballistic — displacement ∝ time, 0.96–1.0 rings per generation, twice the speed of Goucher's glider — because its rule grants states 1 and 2 no survival rows at all: every live cell dies each generation and the pattern exists only by rebirth ahead of itself. The wanderers are super-diffusive but sub-ballistic (exponents 0.58–0.84 against 0.5 for diffusion, 1.0 for ballistic), 31× faster than a random walk over the same span — and two unrelated wanderer rules with visibly different temperaments share the same transport curve — suggesting the exponent is set by the board the two rules share, not by either rule. The two runs that produced impostors were caught by the standard battery: one locked into a period-145 orbit after 128 rings, one died at 48 — full fitness in-patch, exposed cross-radius.

What the control bought

Two numbers and a fact. Rediscovering a glider cost ≈115,000 genome evaluations; the hat and spectre searches of the last two sections spent ≈15,000. Every monotile GA negative might therefore be search-power-bounded — only the exhaustive 2-state census is absolute within its family. And the fact: P3's gliders all exploit its straight de Bruijn ribbons, while the hat and spectre tilings have no straight-rail analogue and smaller vertex neighbourhoods (degrees 6–7 against P3's 7–11). The question is now sharp. The identical pipeline, at the budget rediscovery had just priced, pointed at the monotiles: can directed transport exist without rails?
