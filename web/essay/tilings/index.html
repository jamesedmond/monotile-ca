The board

tilings, and the fifty-year search for an unrepeatable one

The square grid of the last section is itself a tiling of the plane — just not an interesting one. One shape, meeting edge to edge, and above all periodic: slide the whole plane one square over and it lands exactly on itself. That slide is the whole of the glider's immortality proof: four generations return it to its exact shape a square away, on ground the symmetry guarantees identical — checked once, it flies forever. The question of this essay is what happens without the guarantee.

Tilings that cannot repeat took a long time to find. The first aperiodic tile set, Berger's in 1966, needed 20,426 distinct tiles — built by hand, before computers were involved in such things, and yet born from computation: the tiles encode the steps of a Turing machine, and their refusal to repeat is the undecidability of the halting problem made geometric. Robinson got the count down to six in 1971; Roger Penrose to two in 1974 — the kites and darts (his P2 tiling) and the rhombs (P3), both of which a later section returns to.

And there it stood. Whether a single shape could force aperiodicity — the einstein problem, ein Stein, one stone — stayed open for half a century, until an amateur geometer, David Smith, found the 13-sided hat in 2023. It needs mirrored copies; the spectre, found weeks later, does not.

Rings — graph distance — are the yardstick for everything that follows: every patch in this essay is a root tile grown r rings out (its radius), and how far a pattern travels is measured the same way.

Aperiodic does not mean nothing repeats. Any finite patch you can point to — a tile and its ring, a whole neighbourhood — recurs infinitely often across the plane; what never recurs is the arrangement as a whole, and nothing ever tells you where the next occurrence will be. This is local isomorphism, and for a glider it would be the only footing on offer — familiar ground keeps arriving, on an unpredictable schedule, and the pattern must survive whatever comes between.

What makes these boards usable for experiments at all is that every tile has a name. Each tiling is built by substitution — small tiles compose into supertiles, supertiles into supersupertiles, without end — and a tile's address is simply the path upward: which slot of which supertile, of which supersupertile, and so on. The path never terminates, so a finite address must end in a repeating cycle, the way 1/7 does in decimal. The same ancestry sorts every tile into a tile class: two tiles share a class when they sit in the same slot of the same kind of supertile — the same role in the construction, one level up. There are only finitely many roles, so finitely many classes: 17 on the hat, 10 on the spectre. The two-coloring is the coarsest such sorting (hat against anti-hat; on the spectre, Mystic pairs against the rest); the classes are the finest the substitution offers, and later sections will hand a rule a separate table per class. Switch the explorer's lens to classes to see them, or to hierarchy and slide the level: the supertiles materialize out of the chaos, level by level. Neighbourhood is computable from addresses alone — a finite-state machine reads two names and answers whether the tiles touch. The construction is Simon Tatham's (paper, library), and this whole project stands on it. No geometry is consulted; the coordinates you see are for your eyes only.

One more thing to click for: neighbour counts. On the square grid every cell had the same number of neighbours; here the count varies tile by tile — four to six for hats under edge adjacency, four for every anti-hat, up to seven on spectres. No rule can say "exactly my eight neighbours" on a board like this. Rules must speak in counts — semi-totalistic, as in the last section — and the game is ready to begin.
