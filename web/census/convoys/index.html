Convoys

formation pairs, bound pairs and gliders with passengers

The census found fifteen travelling objects that are larger than a single glider. Each one is certified in the same way as the single gliders: a finite set of situations, and on every terrain the tiling can present the rule takes each situation to another live situation in the set. Each one therefore travels forever, in the single gliders' direction and at their speed.

Are these objects gliders that happen to fly close together, or larger objects that are joined? To find out, every state of each object was split into its connected pieces, and each piece was looked up in the certificates of the single gliders. Both answers are right, for different objects, and there is a third kind in between.

The number on each tab is the number of situations in the object's certificate, which is how the census names it. Each viewer flies the selected object in a sliding window for as long as it stays on screen. The heading in the corner of a viewer is measured in that record's own frame, so the same lane can show headings that differ by a multiple of 60°.

Formation pairs

Eight objects are two known gliders flying in formation on parallel rows. In four of the pairs the second glider is about 8 units behind the first and 18 to its side. In the other four the two are level, or a few units from level. The two gliders are apart in most of their states and touch at intervals. While they touch, their cells cannot be divided into two known glider patterns; afterwards the same two gliders separate again.

None of the eight is under hat C's rule. That rule's showers throw out gliders in twos, but none of those pairs could be certified, so the census cannot say whether hat C's rule has a formation pair.

Bound pairs

Two objects under hat A's rule are pairs of gliders that are joined into one connected pattern for much of their cycle: the 441 in 87% of its states and the 561 in 58%. In many of those states the joined pattern, of eight to fourteen cells, has no single glider's pattern inside it. For the rest of the cycle the pair is two intact gliders, which then join again.

Gliders with passengers

Five objects are a known glider with four or five extra cells attached behind it, in every state. The attached cells are never a glider by themselves. The object keeps the single glider's lane and speed. The census found them only under hat B's rule.
